Method

How the calls are made, and how they can fail

Leadtime reads bottlenecks from company revenue and customs data, not from headlines. Every rule is written down and published before we compute its results, and every call goes into a public log that can't be edited.

1. The pressure index

A bottleneck shows up in the numbers before it shows up in the news. When memory runs short, Taiwanese memory makers' monthly revenue and Korea's chip exports rise faster than their own history suggests. Each theme lists the companies and customs codes that sit on its supply chain.

  • Components: Taiwan monthly revenue for the listed companies (MOPS, from 2005), US imports for listed HS6 codes (Census, from 2013), and Korea exports for listed HS codes (Korea Customs).
  • Per component: year-on-year growth of the trailing three-month sum, turned into a z-score against its own history up to that month (at least 24 months, never using the future).
  • C, the pressure level: the average of the available z-scores. Above zero means tighter than usual.
  • M, the momentum: the three-month change in C, using only components present at both dates. This keeps a new data source from creating a fake jump.
  • Timing: each month's data counts only from the date it would really have been public: the 16th of the next month for Taiwan and Korea, two months later for US imports.

2. Calls

The model makes the calls. Nobody at Leadtime overrides them. If we ever disagree, we log that separately and label it as a human view.

  • Memory, IN / OUT (Rule M): stay IN unless pressure is high and falling (C > 0 and M < 0). Tested out of sample, 2008–2017, before any live call was made.
  • Other themes, OVERWEIGHT / UNDERWEIGHT vs a benchmark: overweight when C > 0. A theme gets a call only if this tilt beat always-overweight, out of sample, in the pre-registered window (2008–2017, which needs Taiwan revenue data).
  • Monitoring: every theme that fails or can't be tested yet. We show its pressure readings, but it gets no call and no call alerts.

3. Discovery scanner

The scanner flags any listed Taiwanese company, or any US/Korea trade code, whose sales jump far outside their own pattern. Rule v5: growth z-score ≥ 2, YoY growth ≥ 30%, rising three-month momentum, and revenue above NT$300m (US$50m for trade codes). A company can be flagged at most once every six months.

4. Data sources

All sources are free and public, and the code that fetches them is in the repository.

  • Taiwan MOPS: monthly revenue for every listed company, 2005 on.
  • US Census international trade API: monthly imports by HS6 code, 2013 on.
  • Korea Customs Service trade statistics: monthly exports by HS code.
  • FRED: producer price indexes (e.g. transformers, switchgear).
  • SEC XBRL and UN Comtrade: cross-checks.
  • Yahoo Finance: daily closes for baskets and benchmarks.

5. Pre-registration and the log

Before we test a rule, we write it down, hash it and push it to GitHub. Rules that fail stay public. Each month the calls are appended to signal_log.jsonl, where every entry carries the SHA-256 of the one before it. See the track record.

6. Limits

  • Few live calls: the live log started in September 2026. Backtests aren't a track record.
  • Survivorship: baskets list companies that exist today, which flatters old backtest returns.
  • Taiwan-heavy: the best monthly company data is Taiwanese. Themes without Taiwanese suppliers rely on trade data that only starts in 2013, so most of them stay in Monitoring.
  • Mislabelled codes: customs codes mix products, and an HS code can move for reasons unrelated to AI.
  • No costs: returns ignore trading costs, taxes and currency.

Leadtime is research, not investment advice. It doesn't know your circumstances. Do your own work before you trade.
